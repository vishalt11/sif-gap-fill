\chapter{Introduction\label{cha:chapter1}}
Photosynthesis controls vegetation growth, terrestrial carbon uptake, and the formation of crop biomass, making its observation across large areas important for both carbon-cycle research and agricultural monitoring \cite{guan2016improving,he2020ground}. Conventional reflectance-based vegetation indices mainly describe canopy greenness and structure, whereas solar-induced chlorophyll fluorescence (SIF) is emitted by chlorophyll during photosynthetic energy conversion and provides a more functional indicator of current photosynthetic activity \cite{gensheimer2022convolutional,yu2019high}. Satellite SIF has consequently been used to study gross primary productivity, vegetation stress, and crop productivity, and it can detect heat- or drought-related reductions in photosynthetic activity before equally strong changes appear in greenness indices \cite{qiu2022monitoring,song2018satellite,peng2020assessing}. Current satellite observations, however, involve a spatial-sampling trade-off: OCO-2 provides comparatively small SIF footprints along narrow and widely separated tracks, while TROPOMI provides much more complete coverage at a coarser spatial resolution \cite{yu2019high,gensheimer2022convolutional}. Because SIF is weak relative to reflected sunlight, individual retrievals contain considerable noise, and the spatial or temporal averaging used to improve signal stability also removes detail from heterogeneous agricultural landscapes \cite{yu2019high}. Machine-learning methods can address part of this limitation by combining sparse or coarse SIF observations with spatially complete reflectance, vegetation-index, radiation, environmental, seasonal, and land-cover information \cite{li2019global,zhang2018global,gensheimer2022convolutional}. Products such as SIFoco2\_005 demonstrate that sparse OCO-2 measurements can be reconstructed as spatially continuous fields, while SIFnet shows that a spatial model can combine a parent SIF observation with finer auxiliary rasters to infer sub-grid variation \cite{yu2019high,gensheimer2022convolutional}. Nevertheless, even recent enhanced products commonly provide estimates at approximately 500~m or coarser resolution, which can remain too coarse to isolate individual crops in fragmented European agricultural landscapes \cite{gensheimer2022convolutional,du2025cnsif}. This thesis therefore investigates whether sparse OCO-2 SIF observations over Germany can be linked to Sentinel-2 data and ancillary biophysical, radiation, seasonal, and crop-composition variables represented on a 20~m grid. It compares tabular tree-based models, an area-to-point neural network, and spatial convolutional models, while separately examining how the spatial support and aggregation of OCO-2 supervision affect target stability and prediction accuracy. The resulting 20~m maps are treated as model-derived estimates learned from footprint- or grid-supported observations, rather than as directly observed or independently validated SIF at 20~m resolution. Finally, the enhanced maps are restricted to winter-wheat pixels to test whether crop-pure SIF provides more useful information for Bavarian winter-wheat yield prediction than raw OCO-2 footprints containing mixed crops and other land-cover types.

\section{Motivation}
Crop-specific yield monitoring requires observations that represent vegetation activity across complete agricultural regions and throughout the important stages of the growing season. In preliminary work for this thesis, raw OCO-2 SIF footprints were used to investigate winter-wheat yield prediction. Annual crop-type rasters were used to calculate the proportion of winter wheat within each footprint, and several minimum wheat-coverage thresholds were examined. The retained observations were then converted into seasonal mean SIF values for Bavarian NUTS-3 regions. However, this procedure produced an extremely sparse regional dataset, with approximately 45--80\% of the required values missing depending on the NUTS-3 region, year, and seasonal period. Applying a stricter wheat-coverage threshold improved crop purity but left too few observations for many regions, whereas accepting more footprints increased coverage at the cost of including other crops and land-cover types. Additional spatial or temporal averaging could not fully solve the problem because it further reduced the available detail and the number of distinct observations. This preliminary analysis demonstrated that raw OCO-2 sampling alone is insufficient for spatially complete and crop-specific regional yield analysis.

The problem is closely related to the trade-off between spatial resolution, temporal coverage, and measurement noise in current satellite SIF observations. OCO-2 provides relatively small footprints but only along narrow and widely separated tracks, while spatially complete SIF products generally have much coarser pixels. Even enhanced SIF datasets commonly remain at approximately 500~m to 1~km resolution or coarser \cite{gensheimer2022convolutional,du2025cnsif}. At these scales, one pixel can contain several fields, crop types, and non-agricultural surfaces, particularly in the fragmented agricultural landscape of Bavaria. A finer SIF representation is therefore needed to isolate winter-wheat areas and reduce contamination from neighbouring land-cover types. Sentinel-2 provides spatially complete optical bands and vegetation indices at 10--20~m resolution, which can describe field-scale variation between the sparse OCO-2 tracks. Combining these data with radiation, climatological, seasonal, and crop-composition variables offers a way to estimate SIF in locations and periods without a direct OCO-2 retrieval. This motivates the development of a more spatially and temporally complete SIF representation and the subsequent test of whether crop-pure enhanced SIF is more useful for yield prediction than raw mixed-footprint OCO-2 observations.

\section{Objective}
The primary objective of this thesis is to develop and evaluate a machine-learning workflow for enhancing sparse OCO-2 SIF observations. The workflow combines OCO-2 supervision with high-resolution spectral and vegetation information as well as radiation, climatological, seasonal, and crop-composition predictors. Tabular tree-based models, an area-to-point neural network, and spatial convolutional models are compared to examine how model architecture and input representation affect prediction accuracy and the spatial form of the resulting maps. The study also tests whether aggregating noisy OCO-2 observations provides more stable supervision than modelling individual footprints directly. The workflow is designed so that it can later be applied to generate spatially and temporally complete high-resolution SIF data over a broader German domain. Because complete national multi-year processing is computationally intensive, the thesis focuses on producing and evaluating the modelling approach, data-preparation procedures, and reproducible code rather than generating an exhaustive national product. The proposed approach is compared with the capabilities and limitations of existing SIF reconstruction and downscaling methods reported in the literature. The selected enhanced-SIF model is then integrated into a winter-wheat yield-prediction framework by extracting crop-pure monthly SIF and aggregating it to Bavarian NUTS-3 regions. Finally, the study quantifies whether this crop-pure representation adds predictive value relative to raw OCO-2 footprints with incomplete and mixed vegetation coverage.

\section{Thesis Scope}
The SIF-enhancement study is geographically restricted to selected Sentinel-2 MGRS tiles across Germany rather than wall-to-wall processing of every German pixel. This limitation is necessary because multi-year Sentinel-2 processing is highly data- and computation-intensive, and the required products for 11 MGRS tiles can approach approximately 500~GB before further model-ready processing. The common temporal scope is 2019--2024 because the Sentinel-2 L3A WASP monthly archive used in the thesis begins in 2019 and the required annual crop-type rasters extend through 2024. Although several secondary vegetation, radiation, and climatological datasets cover longer periods, they are restricted to this shared interval to maintain a consistent set of predictor channels. The main SIF-enhancement experiments use available observations and predictor products from February to July, corresponding to the principal vegetation growing period considered in this study. The yield application has a narrower geographic scope and covers Bavarian NUTS-3 regions within selected MGRS tiles, using monthly SIF from March to July, the years 2019--2022 for model development, and 2024 as an independent temporal test year. The year 2023 is excluded from the yield experiment because matching optical predictor coverage is incomplete. Although the enhanced maps are produced on a 20~m grid, their evaluation is performed after aggregation to OCO-2 footprints or 4~km cells, and the thesis therefore does not claim independent validation of individual 20~m SIF pixels.

\section{Purpose and Goal\label{sec:purpose_and_goal}}
The main purpose of this thesis is to bridge the scale gap between sparse OCO-2 SIF observations and the field-scale spatial information required for crop-specific agricultural analysis. It investigates whether high-resolution Sentinel-2 and ancillary predictors can distribute footprint- or grid-supported SIF into spatial patterns that are useful in fragmented German farmland. A related purpose is to determine whether restricting the enhanced estimates to winter-wheat pixels reduces the mixed-cover problem encountered when raw OCO-2 footprints are aggregated for regional yield prediction. The research also aims to clarify how predictor information, spatial context, OCO-2 aggregation, and target preparation influence enhancement performance instead of attributing all differences to model architecture alone. Its methodological contribution is a reproducible modelling and data-preparation workflow that can support later generation of longer and more geographically complete SIF records without claiming that an operational national product has already been produced. Beyond crop-yield estimation, spatially detailed and temporally complete SIF could support the monitoring of GPP, vegetation productivity, phenological development, and physiological responses to drought or heat stress. The agricultural goal is to provide more complete crop-specific photosynthetic information that could strengthen winter-wheat monitoring and early-warning yield applications when combined with weather, soil, and management variables. The resulting maps are nevertheless treated as model-derived estimates constrained by coarser observations rather than as directly measured SIF at 20~m resolution. The intended outcome is therefore an evaluated high-resolution SIF-enhancement framework, a transparent account of its validation limits, and evidence on whether crop-pure enhanced SIF provides a useful direction for future yield research.


\section{Outline\label{sec:outline}}

The remainder of this thesis is organized into six chapters that progress from the theoretical foundations and related research to the study design, experimental findings, and conclusions.

\noindent\textbf{Chapter~\ref{cha:chapter2}} introduces the theoretical background required to understand satellite SIF, its relationship with photosynthesis and GPP, and the relevant remote-sensing predictors. It also explains the modelling concepts, spatial supports, and evaluation metrics used in the study.

\noindent\textbf{Chapter~\ref{cha:chapter3}} reviews existing approaches for SIF reconstruction, gap filling, and spatial downscaling, followed by remote-sensing methods for crop-yield and GPP estimation. The chapter compares their main advantages and limitations and identifies the research gap addressed by this thesis.

\noindent\textbf{Chapter~\ref{cha:chapter4}} defines the problem created by the sparse spatial sampling of OCO-2 and the difficulty of obtaining crop-specific SIF information. It then presents the research questions concerning SIF prediction, model representation, spatial aggregation, geographic generalization, winter-wheat yield estimation, the effect of OCO-2 sample density on field-scale uncertainty, and the transferability of the enhancement framework to other crops.

\noindent\textbf{Chapter~\ref{cha:chapter5}} describes the research design, study area, datasets, preprocessing procedures, and construction of the model-ready representations. It further documents the evaluated model architectures, training and validation strategies, experimental environment, reproducibility measures, and application of the evaluation metrics.

\noindent\textbf{Chapter~\ref{cha:chapter6}} presents and discusses the experimental results for SIF prediction, model architecture, input representation, spatial aggregation, and transfer to previously unseen MGRS tiles. It also evaluates spatial and seasonal performance differences, compares crop-pure and raw SIF for winter-wheat yield prediction, and relates the findings to existing enhancement techniques.

\noindent\textbf{Chapter~\ref{cha:chapter7}} summarizes the principal findings and considers the extent to which the research questions were answered. It also discusses dissemination, practical problems and limitations encountered during the study, and possible directions for extending the SIF-enhancement and crop-monitoring workflow.
